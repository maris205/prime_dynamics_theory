\begin{abstract}
For the integral H\'enon automorphism
\(
H(x,y)=(1-6x^2-y,x)
\)
and the reversing-axis seed \(P_0=(0,0)\), let \(r_p\) be the return period
of \(P_0\) modulo a rational prime \(p\).  RH-362 attached one complete
marked \(r_p\)-cycle to each prime and obtained a locally finite formal
Artin--Mazur product.  We prove that this formal object has a nontrivial
analytic domain.

Reversibility compresses every two-coordinate gcd term to one midpoint
coordinate difference:
\(
a_{2k}=|x_k-x_{k-1}|
\)
and
\(
a_{2k+1}=|x_{k+1}-x_{k-1}|
\).
The escaping negative orbit then satisfies
\(
5b_n^2\le b_{n+1}\le6b_n^2
\),
which yields explicit two-sided bounds and
\(
\log a_n=\Theta(2^{n/2})
\).
For the bouquet fixed-point ledger
\(
T_n=\sum_{p:r_p\mid n}r_p
\),
we obtain the all-order envelope
\[
 T_n\le(\log_2 30)n\,2^{\lceil n/2\rceil-2}.
\]
Consequently
\(
\prod_p(1-z^{r_p})^{-1}
\)
converges normally and is holomorphic and zero-free on
\(
|z|<2^{-1/2}
\).

At every odd prime order \(\ell\), the term \(a_\ell\) has a primitive prime
divisor and
\(
[z^\ell]\log\mathcal Z_0=\omega(a_\ell)\ge1
\).
This is an Euler-exponent and logarithmic-coefficient anchor, not a raw zeta
coefficient: decomposable lower-order cycles already change the coefficient
at order seven.  Finally, the naive Hilbert direct sum
\(
\bigoplus_p zU_p
\)
is noncompact and belongs to no finite Schatten class for \(z\ne0\);
therefore the analytic Euler product is not its ordinary Fredholm
determinant.

The bouquet remains a marked disjoint union across distinct finite fields,
not a full finite-field zeta, Hasse--Weil factor, canonical global H\'enon
operator, or von-Mangoldt trace model.  Gates A--E remain false/open, and no
Hilbert--P\'olya, Riemann-zero, completed-divisor, or RH conclusion is
claimed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Frozen foundation and exact object}

The RH-1--RH-361 foundation remains frozen in four provenance-preserving
volumes \citep{WangRHCorpus2026}.  RH-362 opened an independent
arithmetic-dynamical branch by assigning one marked modular return cycle to
each prime \citep{WangRH3622026}; RH-364 is the immediate numbered baseline
and leaves the original physical route coordinate
\begin{equation}
 \texttt{actual\_same\_clock\_unnormalized\_head\_transport\_open}
 \label{eq:physical-coordinate}
\end{equation}
unchanged \citep{WangRH3642026}.  Nothing below estimates that physical
same-clock defect, supplies a typed \(q/E_{\rm off}\) theorem, or closes the
RH-241 moving noisy envelope.

Fix the integral polynomial automorphism
\begin{equation}
 H(x,y)=(1-6x^2-y,x),
 \qquad
 H^{-1}(u,v)=(v,1-6v^2-u).
 \label{eq:henon-map}
\end{equation}
Let
\begin{equation}
 R(x,y)=(y,x).
 \label{eq:reversor}
\end{equation}
Direct substitution gives
\begin{equation}
 R^2=\operatorname{id},
 \qquad
 RHR=H^{-1}.
 \label{eq:reversibility}
\end{equation}
These identities hold over every commutative ring and hence after reduction
modulo every positive integer \citep{WangPrimeReturns2026}.

For \(P\in\Z^2\), let
\begin{equation}
 r_M(P)=\min\{n\ge1:H^n(P)\equiv P\pmod M\},
 \qquad r_1(P)=1,
 \label{eq:return-rank}
\end{equation}
and, writing \(H^n(P)=(x_n,y_n)\), define
\begin{equation}
 a_n(P)=\gcd(|x_n-x_0|,|y_n-y_0|).
 \label{eq:gcd-sequence-general}
\end{equation}
The locked source theorem is
\begin{equation}
 M\mid a_n(P)
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 r_M(P)\mid n.
 \label{eq:return-divisibility}
\end{equation}
It also proves strong divisibility for every nonperiodic integral orbit
\citep{Silverman2007,WangPrimeReturns2026}.

This paper uses only
\begin{equation}
 P_0=(0,0).
 \label{eq:seed}
\end{equation}
Set \(x_{-1}=x_0=0\) and define
\begin{equation}
 x_{n+1}=1-6x_n^2-x_{n-1}.
 \label{eq:scalar-recurrence}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
 H^n(P_0)=(x_n,x_{n-1}),
 \qquad
 a_n:=a_n(P_0)=\gcd(|x_n|,|x_{n-1}|).
 \label{eq:orbit-indexing}
\end{equation}

\section{Exact reversibility midpoint compression}

The seed lies on the reversing axis: \(RP_0=P_0\).  Thus
\begin{equation}
 H^{-k}P_0=RH^kP_0
 \qquad(k\ge0).
 \label{eq:reversed-orbit}
\end{equation}
This turns a long return into equality of two midpoint states.

\begin{theorem}[Midpoint identities]
\label{thm:midpoint}
For every \(k\ge1\),
\begin{equation}
 a_{2k}=|x_k-x_{k-1}|,
 \label{eq:even-midpoint}
\end{equation}
and for every \(k\ge0\),
\begin{equation}
 a_{2k+1}=|x_{k+1}-x_{k-1}|.
 \label{eq:odd-midpoint}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix a positive modulus \(M\).  Because \(H\) is invertible modulo \(M\),
\begin{align*}
 H^{2k}P_0\equiv P_0\pmod M
 &\Longleftrightarrow
 H^kP_0\equiv H^{-k}P_0\pmod M\\
 &\Longleftrightarrow
 (x_k,x_{k-1})\equiv(x_{k-1},x_k)\pmod M\\
 &\Longleftrightarrow
 M\mid x_k-x_{k-1}.
\end{align*}
Together with \eqref{eq:return-divisibility}, this says that the two
nonnegative integers in \eqref{eq:even-midpoint} have exactly the same
positive divisors, hence are equal.

Likewise,
\begin{align*}
 H^{2k+1}P_0\equiv P_0\pmod M
 &\Longleftrightarrow
 H^{k+1}P_0\equiv H^{-k}P_0\pmod M\\
 &\Longleftrightarrow
 (x_{k+1},x_k)\equiv(x_{k-1},x_k)\pmod M\\
 &\Longleftrightarrow
 M\mid x_{k+1}-x_{k-1},
\end{align*}
which proves \eqref{eq:odd-midpoint}.  The convention \(x_{-1}=0\) includes
\(k=0\).
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The theorem is specific to the reversing-axis seed \(P_0\).  It is not a
height theorem for every integral point and is not inferred from the
finite return-rank tables.
\end{remark}

\section{Quadratic escape and the two-sided height scale}

The first scalar values are
\begin{equation}
 x_0=0,\qquad x_1=1,\qquad x_2=-5,\qquad x_3=-150.
 \label{eq:first-x-values}
\end{equation}
For \(n\ge2\), put
\begin{equation}
 b_n=-x_n.
 \label{eq:b-definition}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Quadratic height recursion and closed bounds]
\label{thm:height}
The sequence \(b_n\) is positive and strictly increasing.  It satisfies
\begin{equation}
 b_2=5,\qquad b_3=150=6b_2^2,
 \label{eq:b-bases}
\end{equation}
and, for \(n\ge3\),
\begin{equation}
 b_{n+1}=6b_n^2-b_{n-1}-1.
 \label{eq:b-exact}
\end{equation}
For every \(n\ge2\),
\begin{equation}
 5b_n^2\le b_{n+1}\le6b_n^2.
 \label{eq:b-quadratic}
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
 5^{\,2^{n-1}-1}
 \le b_n\le
 \frac{30^{\,2^{n-2}}}{6}.
 \label{eq:b-closed}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The bases follow from \eqref{eq:scalar-recurrence}.  Suppose
\(b_n>b_{n-1}\ge5\).  Because the variables are integral,
\(
b_{n-1}+1\le b_n
\).
Equation \eqref{eq:scalar-recurrence} gives \eqref{eq:b-exact}, and hence
\[
 b_{n+1}
 \ge6b_n^2-b_n
 >b_n.
\]
This proves positivity and strict increase by induction from
\((b_2,b_3)=(5,150)\).  The upper inequality in \eqref{eq:b-quadratic} is
immediate from \eqref{eq:b-exact}; for the lower inequality,
\[
 b_{n-1}+1\le b_n\le b_n^2
\]
gives
\(
b_{n+1}\ge5b_n^2
\).
The case \(n=2\) follows directly from \eqref{eq:b-bases}.

Multiplying the lower recurrence by five gives
\(
5b_{n+1}\ge(5b_n)^2
\),
and multiplying the upper recurrence by six gives
\(
6b_{n+1}\le(6b_n)^2
\).
Iterating from \(5b_2=25\) and \(6b_2=30\) yields
\eqref{eq:b-closed}.
\end{proof}

Strict increase proves again that the integral orbit of \(P_0\) is
nonperiodic.  In particular every \(a_n\) is positive.

\begin{theorem}[Explicit two-sided bounds for the gcd sequence]
\label{thm:gcd-height}
The first values are
\begin{equation}
 a_1=a_2=1,\qquad
 a_3=5,\quad a_4=6,\quad a_5=151.
 \label{eq:a-bases}
\end{equation}
Let \(n\ge6\) and \(m=\lceil n/2\rceil\).  Then
\begin{equation}
 \frac{24}{25}b_m\le a_n<b_m.
 \label{eq:a-sharp-comparison}
\end{equation}
Uniformly for every \(n\ge3\),
\begin{equation}
 4\,5^{\,2^{m-1}-2}
 \le a_n\le
 \frac{30^{\,2^{m-2}}}{5},
 \qquad m=\left\lceil\frac n2\right\rceil.
 \label{eq:a-closed}
\end{equation}
Thus
\begin{equation}
 \log a_n=\Theta(2^{n/2}).
 \label{eq:log-height}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The bases follow from theorem~\ref{thm:midpoint}.  If \(n=2m\ge6\), then
\[
 a_n=b_m-b_{m-1}.
\]
If \(n=2m-1\ge7\), then
\[
 a_n=b_m-b_{m-2}.
\]
In either case the subtracted term is at most \(b_{m-1}\).  Since
\[
 b_m\ge5b_{m-1}^2\ge25b_{m-1},
\]
equation \eqref{eq:a-sharp-comparison} follows.

The three special values in \eqref{eq:a-bases}, together with
\eqref{eq:a-sharp-comparison}, give the uniform comparison
\begin{equation}
 \frac45b_m\le a_n\le\frac65b_m
 \qquad(n\ge3).
 \label{eq:a-uniform-comparison}
\end{equation}
Combining this with \eqref{eq:b-closed} proves \eqref{eq:a-closed}.
Finally \(2^{m-1}\) is comparable to \(2^{n/2}\), proving
\eqref{eq:log-height}.
\end{proof}

\section{The all-order bouquet trace envelope}

For a rational prime \(p\), write \(r_p=r_p(P_0)\), and let
\begin{equation}
 \cO_p=\{P_0,H_pP_0,\ldots,H_p^{r_p-1}P_0\}
 \label{eq:marked-cycle}
\end{equation}
be the complete marked cycle in \(\mathbb F_p^2\).  Let \(U_p\) be its cyclic
permutation matrix.  Then
\begin{equation}
 \Tr(U_p^n)=r_p\mathbf 1_{r_p\mid n},
 \qquad
 \det(I-zU_p)=1-z^{r_p}.
 \label{eq:local-cycle}
\end{equation}

Define the countable marked disjoint union
\begin{equation}
 \cX_0=\bigsqcup_p\cO_p
 \label{eq:bouquet}
\end{equation}
and let \(F_0\) act on each component as the cyclic restriction of \(H_p\):
\begin{equation}
 F_0|_{\cO_p}=H_p|_{\cO_p}.
 \label{eq:bouquet-map}
\end{equation}
Define also the rank multiplicities
\begin{equation}
 c_d=\#\{p:r_p=d\}.
 \label{eq:rank-multiplicity}
\end{equation}
The return-divisibility theorem implies that \(c_d\) is finite for every
\(d\): every such prime divides the fixed positive integer \(a_d\).

\begin{theorem}[Exact fixed-point ledger and all-order envelope]
\label{thm:trace-envelope}
For every \(n\ge1\), the fixed-point set of the \(n\)-th bouquet iterate is
finite and
\begin{equation}
 T_n:=\#\Fix(F_0^n)
 =\sum_{p:r_p\mid n}r_p
 =\sum_{d\mid n}d\,c_d
 =\sum_{p\mid a_n}r_p.
 \label{eq:trace-ledger}
\end{equation}
Moreover \(T_1=T_2=0\), and for every \(n\ge1\),
\begin{equation}
 T_n\le
 (\log_2 30)n\,2^{\lceil n/2\rceil-2}.
 \label{eq:trace-envelope}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Equation \eqref{eq:local-cycle} and finiteness of the set \(r_p\le n\) give
the first three expressions in \eqref{eq:trace-ledger}.  Equation
\eqref{eq:return-divisibility} gives the last.

If \(p\mid a_n\), then \(r_p\mid n\), hence \(r_p\le n\).  Therefore
\begin{equation}
 T_n\le n\,\omega(a_n),
 \label{eq:T-omega}
\end{equation}
where \(\omega\) counts distinct prime factors.  Since
\(
2^{\omega(a_n)}\le\rad(a_n)\le a_n
\),
\begin{equation}
 \omega(a_n)\le\log_2a_n.
 \label{eq:omega-log}
\end{equation}
For \(n\ge3\), theorem~\ref{thm:gcd-height} gives
\[
 \log_2a_n
 \le2^{\lceil n/2\rceil-2}\log_2 30-\log_2 5,
\]
which proves \eqref{eq:trace-envelope}.  The terms \(a_1=a_2=1\) give
\(T_1=T_2=0\), so the same displayed envelope holds at the two base orders.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Data type]
\label{rem:trace-data-type}
The term \emph{trace ledger} refers to the finite sum of local cycle traces,
or equivalently the bouquet fixed-point count.  It is not yet the
Hilbert-space trace of a trace-class global operator.
Section~\ref{sec:naive-operator} proves the precise obstruction.
\end{remark}

\section{A strict zero-free disk}

The RH-362 formal Artin--Mazur product is
\begin{equation}
 \cZ_0(z)
 =\exp\left(\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{T_n}{n}z^n\right)
 =\prod_p(1-z^{r_p})^{-1}.
 \label{eq:formal-zeta}
\end{equation}
RH-362 asserted this coefficientwise, without a positive analytic radius.

\begin{theorem}[Normal convergence and zero-free disk]
\label{thm:analytic-disk}
For every
\begin{equation}
 0\le r<2^{-1/2},
 \label{eq:strict-radius}
\end{equation}
one has
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{n\ge1}\frac{T_n}{n}r^n
 \le
 (\log_2 30)\frac{r^3+r^4}{1-2r^2}.
 \label{eq:log-majorant}
\end{equation}
Consequently the logarithmic series and Euler product in
\eqref{eq:formal-zeta} converge locally normally on
\begin{equation}
 |z|<2^{-1/2}.
 \label{eq:analytic-disk}
\end{equation}
They define a holomorphic zero-free function \(\cZ_0\) there, and
\(\cZ_0^{-1}\) is also holomorphic and zero-free.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The base traces vanish.  The odd and even parts of
\eqref{eq:trace-envelope} give
\begin{align*}
 \sum_{n\ge1}\frac{T_n}{n}r^n
 &\le(\log_2 30)
 \left(
  \sum_{j\ge0}2^jr^{2j+3}
  +\sum_{j\ge0}2^jr^{2j+4}
 \right),
\end{align*}
which is \eqref{eq:log-majorant}.

For \(|z|\le r<2^{-1/2}\), Tonelli's theorem and
\eqref{eq:trace-ledger} give
\begin{equation}
 \sum_p\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{|z|^{jr_p}}j
 =\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{T_n}{n}|z|^n<\infty.
 \label{eq:tonelli-log}
\end{equation}
Thus the analytic logarithms
\(
\sum_{j\ge1}z^{jr_p}/j
\)
sum absolutely and uniformly on compact subdisks.  Exponentiating their sum
and its negative proves normal convergence and the two zero-free claims.
\end{proof}

The inequality is strict at the boundary: the denominator in
\eqref{eq:log-majorant} vanishes when \(r=2^{-1/2}\).  No assertion is made
there.

\begin{corollary}[Origin Taylor-radius bracket]
\label{cor:radius-bracket}
Let \(R_{\log}\) and \(R_{\cZ}\) be the origin Taylor radii of
\(\log\cZ_0\) and \(\cZ_0\), respectively.  Then
\begin{equation}
 2^{-1/2}\le R_{\log}\le1,
 \qquad
 2^{-1/2}\le R_{\cZ}\le1.
 \label{eq:radius-bracket}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The lower bounds are theorem~\ref{thm:analytic-disk}.  The upper bounds
follow from the prime-order anchors in theorem~\ref{thm:prime-anchors}:
the logarithmic coefficient is at least one at infinitely many orders, and
the coefficients of the Euler product are nonnegative with the raw
coefficient at least the primitive contribution.
\end{proof}

This is a bracket for the origin power-series radii.  It is not an exact
radius, a meromorphic-continuation theorem, or a natural-boundary theorem.

\section{Odd-prime primitive anchors and the raw-coefficient firewall}

\begin{definition}
A prime \(p\) is a primitive prime divisor of \(a_n\) when
\(
p\mid a_n
\)
and \(p\nmid a_m\) for every \(1\le m<n\).
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Primitive anchors at every odd prime order]
\label{thm:prime-anchors}
Let \(\ell\) be an odd rational prime.  Then \(a_\ell>1\), and
\begin{equation}
 \{p:p\mid a_\ell\}
 =\{p:r_p=\ell\}.
 \label{eq:prime-anchor-set}
\end{equation}
Every distinct prime divisor of \(a_\ell\) is primitive.  In particular,
\begin{equation}
 c_\ell=\omega(a_\ell)\ge1,
 \qquad
 T_\ell=\ell c_\ell,
 \qquad
 [z^\ell]\log\cZ_0(z)=c_\ell.
 \label{eq:prime-anchor-ledger}
\end{equation}
The number of primitive bouquet cycles of length \(\ell\) is exactly
\(c_\ell\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The lower bound in theorem~\ref{thm:gcd-height} gives \(a_\ell\ge4\), so
\(a_\ell\) has a prime divisor.  Equation \eqref{eq:return-divisibility}
gives
\[
 p\mid a_\ell
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 r_p\mid\ell.
\]
No prime has \(r_p=1\), because that would imply \(p\mid a_1=1\).
Since \(\ell\) is prime, the only remaining divisor is \(r_p=\ell\), proving
\eqref{eq:prime-anchor-set}.  If such a \(p\) divided \(a_m\) for some
\(m<\ell\), then \(\ell=r_p\mid m\), a contradiction.  The first two
identities in \eqref{eq:prime-anchor-ledger} follow.

Finally,
\[
 [z^\ell]\log\cZ_0=\frac{T_\ell}{\ell}=c_\ell.
\]
Equivalently, M\"obius inversion of the fixed-point ledger gives primitive
cycle count
\(
(T_\ell-T_1)/\ell=c_\ell
\).
\end{proof}

The theorem does not assert that \(a_\ell\) is squarefree.  The symbol
\(\omega\) counts distinct factors, not factors with multiplicity.  It also
does not imply a primitive divisor at any composite order.

\begin{proposition}[The anchor is not the raw zeta coefficient]
\label{prop:raw-firewall}
Write
\begin{equation}
 \cZ_0(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}q_nz^n.
 \label{eq:raw-coefficients}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
 nq_n=\sum_{k=1}^nT_kq_{n-k},
 \qquad q_0=1.
 \label{eq:zeta-recurrence}
\end{equation}
The exact finite ledger begins
\begin{equation}
 (q_0,\ldots,q_7)=(1,0,0,1,2,1,2,3).
 \label{eq:first-zeta-coefficients}
\end{equation}
At the prime order seven,
\begin{equation}
 c_7=1,
 \qquad
 [z^7]\log\cZ_0=1,
 \qquad
 [z^7]\cZ_0=3.
 \label{eq:order-seven-firewall}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Differentiating \eqref{eq:formal-zeta} and comparing coefficients gives
\eqref{eq:zeta-recurrence}.  The midpoint identities give
\[
 a_3=5,\qquad a_4=6,\qquad a_7=134989.
\]
Direct exact factorization gives rank multiplicities
\(
c_3=1,c_4=2,c_7=1
\);
the artifact independently checks the return ranks and the primality of
\(134989\).  Thus
\[
 (T_1,\ldots,T_7)=(0,0,3,8,5,9,7).
\]
Substitution in \eqref{eq:zeta-recurrence} proves
\eqref{eq:first-zeta-coefficients} and \eqref{eq:order-seven-firewall}.
The two extra order-seven contributions are decomposable products of one
length-three and one of the two length-four cycles.
\end{proof}

\section{Naive direct-sum obstruction}
\label{sec:naive-operator}

Let
\begin{equation}
 \cH_0=\bigoplus_p\C^{r_p},
 \qquad
 U=\bigoplus_pU_p,
 \qquad
 B_z=zU.
 \label{eq:naive-operator}
\end{equation}
The operator \(U\) is unitary.  Formally, its finite blocks have the local
determinants used in \eqref{eq:formal-zeta}.  Ordinary Fredholm determinants,
however, require trace-ideal hypotheses \citep{Simon2005}.

\begin{theorem}[Noncompactness and the Fredholm firewall]
\label{thm:noncompact}
For every \(z\ne0\), the operator \(B_z\) is noncompact and belongs to no
finite Schatten class \(S_q\).  For every \(n\ge1\), \(U^n\) is not trace
class, so \(T_n\) is not its Hilbert-space trace.  Consequently
\(\cZ_0^{-1}\) is not the ordinary Fredholm determinant
\(\det_F(I-B_z)\) of this naive direct sum.

For \(|z|<1\), the operator \(I-B_z\) is nevertheless invertible.
Noncompactness is not a non-Fredholmness statement.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Choose one unit vector \(e_p\) in each prime block.  The vectors
\(
B_ze_p
\)
are mutually orthogonal and all have norm \(|z|\).  They therefore have no
convergent subsequence when \(z\ne0\), proving noncompactness.

Every singular value of \(U_p\) is one.  Hence the partial \(S_q\) burden of
\(B_z\) over any finite prime set \(S\) is
\begin{equation}
 |z|^q\sum_{p\in S}r_p.
 \label{eq:partial-schatten}
\end{equation}
This diverges as \(S\) exhausts the primes, so no finite \(S_q\) membership
holds.  The same observation with \(z=1\) shows that every \(U^n\) has
infinitely many singular values equal to one and is not trace class.
Thus the finite block sum in \eqref{eq:trace-ledger} is a fixed-point ledger,
not a global operator trace.

Finally, if \(|z|<1\), then \(\|B_z\|=|z|<1\), so the Neumann series
\(
\sum_{j\ge0}B_z^j
\)
inverts \(I-B_z\).
\end{proof}

This theorem excludes only the naive constant-weight cycle direct sum.
It is not a nonexistence theorem for every compact realization of the same
scalar germ.

\section{Conclusion}

The marked return bouquet has more structure than its original formal
definition suggested.  Reversibility halves the orbit depth of the gcd
problem, and the escaping midpoint height pays an explicit all-order
fixed-point budget.  This produces a genuine zero-free analytic germ without
inserting prime weights or changing local clocks.

The same argument also exposes the boundary.  Prime order forces new
divisors because the only possible earlier rank is one, but composite order
does not.  The scalar Euler product converges even though the most immediate
global cycle operator is noncompact.  The shortest new arithmetic problem is
therefore a genuine composite-order primitive-divisor theorem; the shortest
operator problem is an intrinsic compact globalization rather than another
marked direct sum.  The original physical same-clock route remains open and
unchanged.
